The commission math is factual: your revenue × the rate. Rates are indicative and editable: Uber Eats ~25-35%, Deliveroo ~25-30% (indicative ranges, public sources, 2024). “Employer-cost SMIC”: the monthly employer cost of a full-time SMIC (the French minimum wage) — gross SMIC of €1,867.02 as of January 1, 2026 (service-public.gouv.fr), residual employer contributions included (~€1,933). An order of magnitude, not an invoice.

On a delivered order, the commission (~30%) weighs up to 10× your net margin (~3%). You do the work, they cash in.

~3 % is the average net margin of a restaurant — far less than the ~30% commission taken on every delivered order.
~8 000 restaurant businesses in insolvency in France in 2024 — that's +80% in two years.

Sources: ~3% average net margin (Observatoire Fiducial, UMIH/GHR/SNARR press release, June 2025); 2024 insolvencies and +80% over two years (Altares 2024, via L'Hôtellerie-Restauration/GHR). An insolvency = safeguard proceedings, receivership or liquidation; about 2 in 3 end in liquidation.

“I don't care, I just pass the commission on in my prices.”

That's what a lot of owners tell themselves. But your prices swell, customers order less — or somewhere else. Those lost orders? You never see them.

Passing the commission on doesn't cancel it. It just makes your revenue pay for it.

And if you do pass it on? Look:

Your online prices swell noticeably. Customers compare, find you more expensive, and order less — or somewhere else. Your margin looks safe on paper, but the lost sales? You never see them.

You lose on both fronts: your prices still go up (so less volume), and you swallow the other half (so less margin).

Your customers aren't your customers.

No way to build loyalty: no usable loyalty scheme, and you end up depending on the platform to get orders at all.

You don't set your prices.

To absorb their commission, you inflate your online prices. And you're stuck with forced promotions and algorithm changes you depend on — which hit your ranking.

“0% commission — so what do you live on?”

Fair question — and here's the answer, in plain words.

First, the essential: you, the restaurateur, never pay a commission on your dishes — €0, no matter what. The service isn't funded on your back.

It's funded by a customer contribution at a pay-what-you-want price, added to their order: everyone gives what they want to keep a fair alternative alive. It's a bet — that informed customers would rather support their local businesses than a multinational.

A customer doesn't contribute this time? That's fine: no contribution on that order, and still 0% commission for you. Card payment fees are the standard fees of any online payment — never a percentage on your dishes, never a surcharge added for the customer.

🛟 A course that won't drift

An alternative that can never turn into a tollbooth

Captain.Food starts from a simple conviction: digital tools should serve the public good, not be a tollbooth in the hands of a few tech giants. The problem isn't that they make money — it's that once you depend on them, they can squeeze, and nothing stops them.

So we're building in safeguards that will hold. We're aiming for accreditation as a solidarity-based social utility enterprise (ESUS, a French legal status) — a legal framework that caps profit-taking and locks in the mission — with a cooperative (SCIC) as our destination, where restaurateurs, riders and citizens will have a say. The goal: to be, by design, unable to become what we're fighting.

And so you don't have to take our word for it: we're opening our books in public on Open Collective — every euro received and spent, meant to be visible to everyone. Radical transparency, not a slogan.

Our horizon: for Captain.Food to be a digital commons, not a private asset. The code is open — on GitHub — under the Captain.Food Coopyleft license (a copyleft based on AGPL v3, in the spirit of CoopCycle), whose commercial use is reserved for social and solidarity economy structures — cooperatives and non-profits. Our course: getting it recognized as a digital public good (DPG) — so it's verifiable, and above all replicable: other cities, other cooperatives will be able to take it up. A tool for the common good is meant to be shared — not sold off. Read the manifesto →

The second fight: riders, paid decently

In the gig economy, the rider is a variable to squeeze — precarious, unprotected. We made the opposite choice: pay our riders better than anyone, here in Tours, with the ambition, in time, to hire them as employees. A fair service starts with people treated right.

The questions we get asked

Everything restaurateurs ask us — in plain words, no dodging.

It's free for me — so what do you live on?

For you: 0% commission on your dishes, free signup, no matter what. The service is funded by a pay-what-you-want customer contribution, added to their order in full transparency (€0 is fine, no judgment) — never by you. Card payment fees are the standard banking fees of any online payment, never a surcharge added for the customer. See the full details, worked example included.

What about what my customers pay at the till?

Whatever goes through your usual till (on site, at the counter), we don't touch: it's free, full stop — outside our flow. The contribution only applies to orders paid online through Captain.Food.

And the cost of delivery — who'll pay for it?

Delivery will be a future channel, not active yet. The idea: pay the rider properly (we're aiming for at least €7 per ride). You'd only chip in on your delivered orders, up to what your margin allows — you'd set your floor, and we'd never go below it. The customer would pay the difference, transparently. Never a commission on your dishes. See the math in detail, worked example included.

Do I have to leave Uber Eats / Deliveroo?

No. Captain.Food comes on top, with no exclusivity. Many restaurants keep both and see Captain.Food as their direct channel — the one they control.

There are no customers on the platform yet — why sign up now?

Because it costs you nothing and you give up no exclusivity. You get listed, you recover your customer base from your first direct orders, and you shape the tool while we're starting out. The first ones aboard are the ones who weigh the most.

How do I get my customers back?

Every order placed directly on your page belongs to you: you get access to your customers' contact details (with their consent) to build loyalty yourself. Unlike the big platforms, we don't confiscate your customer base.

What's an ESUS company?

ESUS stands for “entreprise solidaire d'utilité sociale” — a solidarity-based social utility enterprise, a French accreditation that legally caps profit-taking and requires a social purpose. It's the course we're aiming for — a guarantee, in time, that Captain.Food can never drift into what we denounce.

What about ordering at the table (QR code)?

Two cases. If the customer pays at the till or on site, it's outside our flow: 100% free, we don't touch it. If they pay online via the QR code, a pay-what-you-want contribution can be added, just like for an online order. In every case, under your name, and 0% commission on your dishes.

How many languages do you speak — for my customers, for me, for the riders?

17, to start with — the ones spoken around us. This page already speaks them all (try the switcher at the top). And the product will hold the same course: your customers' ordering site, your restaurateur space and, in time, the riders' app. Everyone in their own language — your customer orders in Thai or Arabic, you work in yours.
